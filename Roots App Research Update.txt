   1. **Zezuru:** Centered around Harare and the Mashonaland provinces. This forms the baseline for standard written Shona.
   2. **Karanga:** Based in Masvingo and the Midlands. Historically the largest cluster with deep connections to the Great Zimbabwe kingdom.
   3. **Manyika:** Centered in Manicaland (the eastern Highlands). Features unique vocal intonations and specific regional naming traits.
   4. **Korekore:** Found in northern Zimbabwe (Mashonaland Central/West, stretching down to the Zambezi Valley).
   5. **Ndau:** Located in southeastern Manicaland (Chipinge/Chimanimani). It has highly distinct linguistic structures influenced by Nguni migrations.
 * **The Matabeleland & Western Clusters:**
   6.  **Ndebele:** Driven by the historical 19th-century Khumalo migration from the Zulu kingdom, settling across western and central Zimbabwe.
   7.  **Kalanga:** An ancient group in western Zimbabwe that predates the Ndebele arrival but shares heavy geographic and cultural ties today.
### 2. The Totem Identity Infrastructure
A totem is an unalterable spiritual and biological identification card inherited from the father's side. It prevents inner-clan marriage and traces ancestry back a thousand years.
 * **Shona Mechanics (*Mutupo* + *Chidawo*):**
   * The **Mutupo** is the main category (usually an animal, bird, or organ, like *Moyo* / Heart, *Shumba* / Lion, *Soko* / Monkey).
   * The **Chidawo** is the sub-clan praise name that isolates the specific lineage branch.
   * *App Logic:* If a user inputs *Moyo*, the app must prompt a second mandatory field for their *Chidawo* (e.g., *Moyondizvo*, *Chirandu*, *Sinyoro*). Two *Moyondizvo* cannot marry, but a *Moyo Sinyoro* and a *Moyo Chirandu* might have historical clearance depending on the region.
 * **Ndebele Mechanics (*Isibongo* + *Izithakazelo*):**
   * The **Isibongo** functions as the modern surname (e.g., *Khumalo*, *Ndlovu*, *Dube*).
   * The **Izithakazelo** are the specific ancestral praise names used to address someone with deep respect.

 * **Ndebele Example (*Khumalo* - Royal House):** Address them using their *Izithakazelo*: *“Mntungwa, Khumalo, Mzilankatha kaXaba, Lobengula!”*

### 5. Customary Proverbs (*Tsumo* & *Izaga*)
Traditional courts (*Matare*) run completely on proverbs. They are used by village heads (*Sabhuku*) and chiefs to resolve land, inheritance, and marriage disputes without causing permanent fractures in the family tree.
 * **Shona *Tsumo* Example:** *"Chara chimwe hachitswanyi hinda."* (One thumb cannot crush a louse).
   * *App Relevance:* This is the ultimate family tree motto. It means an individual cannot survive in isolation; identity requires a collective network.
 * **Ndebele *Izaga* Example:** *"Kakho mfula ungabo gubha."* (There is no river that does not dig its own channel).
   * *App Relevance:* Every ancestral line carves out its own distinct historical footprint.
### 6. Totem Taboos (*Miko* / *Izila*)
This is the operational safety logic of the entire kinship network. Every single totem comes with a strict dietary restriction: **you are strictly forbidden from eating your totem animal.**
 * **The Consequence:** Breaking a taboo (*Kutyora miko*) is traditionally believed to bring spiritual bad luck, physical illness, or cause your teeth to fall out.

Shona Marriage:
The confusion during Shona marriage negotiations (*Roora / Lobola*) almost always stems from two things: families mixing up the **chronological order** of the steps, or failing to separate **what belongs to the mother (*Ambuya*)** versus **what belongs to the father (*Tezvara*)**.
In Shona custom, *Roora* is a legalistic process. If the groom’s family (*Vakwasha*) does step 4 before step 2, or speaks out of turn, they are slapped with immediate fines (*Zvirehwa*).
Here is the complete, chronological breakdown of how a Shona *Roora* day is structured, who gets what, and the modern financial expectations.
## The Key Players
Before anyone sits down, you must understand the structural roles:
 * **The Munyai (Go-Between):** The most important person. The groom never speaks; the *Munyai* (negotiator representing the groom) does all the talking and handles the money.
 * **The Vatete (Paternal Aunt):** Represents the bride. She sits with the bride's family but acts as the ultimate mediator, protecting the bride’s interests and confirming consent.
 * **The Groomsmen/Delegation:** Sit quietly behind the *Munyai*. They only speak if the *Munyai* explicitly asks them to help count money or carry items.
## The Chronological Step-by-Step Process
Because doing these steps out of order results in steep financial penalties, your app must enforce this exact sequence:
 1. Kunyora Tsamba & Vhuramuromo
   Weeks Before the Event
   The groom's side sends a formal letter requesting a negotiation date. This is accompanied by a small fee called Vhuramuromo ("mouth opener" to allow the bride's family to open and read the letter).
 2. Zvirango / Entry Protocol Fees
   The Morning of the Roora
   The Vakwasha arrive at the homestead but cannot just walk in. They sit outside or in a designated room and pay small incremental cash fees through the Vatete to proceed: Ndinoreva muromo (permission to speak), Kupinda mumusha (gate entry), and Makadiniko (greeting the elders).
 3. Zvamai (Satisfying the Mother)
   Mid-Morning Phase
   Custom dictates that the mother must be honored and compensated for raising the bride before the father says a single word. This includes Mbonano (fee to physically see the mother), Mafuta eAmbuya (money for body lotion), and Matwiro (compensating her for pounding grain for the bride).
 4. Rusambo (The Core Dowry)
   The Main Negotiation
   This is the most critical structural node. Rusambo is the actual price paid to the father's side that legally binds the marriage. It is negotiated heavily in cash and traditionally signifies the transfer of marital rights.
 5. Danga (The Cattle Portion)
   Afternoon Phase
   The father demands a specific number of cattle (traditionally 5 to 9 cows). One must be a live dairy cow for the mother (Mombe yeHumai). The rest can be charged as a cash equivalent per beast based on local market rates.
 6. Zvatezvara neMbatya (Parental Wardrobes)
   Late Afternoon Phase
   The final material demands. The groom's family must provide complete, brand-new outfits (suits, shoes, hats for the father; dresses, wraps, blankets for the mother and Vatete), or the cash equivalent to buy them.
 7. Madyo / Majaka (The Welcoming Feast)
   Evening Celebration
   Once all primary fees are accepted, the bride's family cooks a massive feast funded by a small fee paid by the groom (Mari yemafuta / cooking gas/oil fee). The groom is officially introduced, and the two families eat together as one.

## Important Customary Nuances to Account For

Ndebeke marriage:
Just like the Shona *Roora* process, the Ndebele marriage system (*Amalobolo*) is deeply structured and procedural. However, the internal logic shifts dramatically in terms of **who holds the real negotiating power** and **how cattle are calculated**.
If you try to negotiate an Ndebele marriage using Shona protocols, the talks will stall immediately. The biggest structural difference is that in Ndebele custom, the maternal side of the family holds massive veto power, and the negotiations are heavily centered around the physical or symbolic transfer of a herd (*Ikhazi*).
## The Key Players
The table setup looks similar to a Shona negotiation, but the titles and underlying authorities are entirely distinct:
 * **Ukhula (The Go-Between):** The equivalent of the Shona *Munyai*. He is the diplomat representing the groom's family (*Abayeni*). He must know the bride's family history and *Izithakazelo* (praise names) perfectly.
 * **Omalume (The Maternal Uncles):** **This is the most critical structural shift.** In Shona custom, the father and paternal aunts (*Vatete*) run the show. In Ndebele custom, your maternal uncle (*Umalume*) must be present. He represents the bloodline that birthed the bride, and he has a dedicated share of the dowry that cannot be negotiated away.
 * **Abomama (The Mothers/Aunts):** They sit in an adjacent room or section, receiving specific gifts and checking the quality of physical goods brought by the groom's side.
## The Chronological Step-by-Step Process
Ndebele negotiations move through a strict legal timeline. Doing these out of order or failing to complete a stage can void the customary validity of the union.
 1. Isicelo (The Formal Request)
   Months Before Negotiations
   The groom's family sends an official letter to the bride's father requesting a date to sit down. This letter is accompanied by Isivulamulomo ("mouth opener" cash) so the family will agree to host the delegation.
 2. Ukungena (Gate Entry Protocols)
   Arrival Morning
   The groom's delegation (Abayeni) arrives at the gate. They do not enter until they pay a small fee to clear the pathway. Once inside the yard, they pay Ikhangaziwe ("let me be known"), which introduces the groom's family to the household ancestors.
 3. Ukubona Amehlo (Meeting the Parents)
   Mid-Morning Phase
   Before discussing cattle, the groom's side must pay a direct cash fee to look the parents in the eyes and state their intentions clearly. This is accompanied by small respect tokens for the bride's father.
 4. Amalobolo / Ikhazi (The Cattle Count)
   The Core Negotiation
   The main architectural node. The bride's family charges a specific number of cattle (the Ikhazi), typically ranging from 5 to 10 beasts. The value is discussed in terms of actual live cows, but settled using a mixed matrix of live animals and cash equivalents based on current regional livestock markets.
 5. Inkomo kaMama (The Mother's Cow)
   The Maternal Anchor
   A mandatory, non-negotiable live heifer or dairy cow is set aside specifically for the bride's mother. This matches the Shona Mombe yeHumai and represents the mother's role in raising and breastfeeding the bride.
 6. Izibizo (The Family Wardrobe & Demands)
   Afternoon Phase
   Once the cattle count is locked down, the bride’s family presents a physical manifest of required material gifts. This includes complete outfits for the parents, heavy blankets (Amacansi), and a very specific fee or coat for the Umalume (Maternal Uncle).
 7. Ukwamukelwa (The Welcoming Feast)
   Evening Conclusion
   The final cash token is paid to allow the families to mix. A beast or goat is slaughtered to seal the contract, and the groom is formally brought into the room to be introduced to his new in-laws over a shared traditional meal.

Shona customary law uses a horizontal, generational system known as **Collateral Succession**. Leadership alternates between distinct lineages (ruling houses) descended from the original founding chief, prioritizing generational seniority over direct father-to-son inheritance.
### 1. The Rotational Matrix of Ruling Houses (*Dzimba*)

```

[House 1: Chiwara] ──(Moves Horizontally)──> [House 2: Museyamwa] ──(Moves Horizontally)──> [House 3: Serima]
                                                                                                    │

GENERATION 2 (The Cousins)                                             ▼

```
 * **The Horizontal Rule:** The throne passes from brother to brother across the entire first generation until all eligible brothers are deceased.
 * **The Vertical Drop:** Only when the last brother of Generation 1 dies does the title drop down to Generation 2. It resets at the eldest surviving son of the senior house (Chiwara's line) and repeats the horizontal rotation among the cousins.

## Customary Rules for Lineage Drift, Seniority, and Dissolution

### Out-of-Wedlock Births and Totem Drift
Under Shona and Ndebele custom, a child born outside of marriage does not automatically inherit the biological father's totem. Instead, they are placed in a "Maternal Vault" state, taking the totem of the maternal grandfather.
To transfer custody and shift the child's totem to the biological father's line (**Totem Drift**), the father must settle specific customary payments with the mother's family.
| Ledger Code | Customary Payment | Recipient | Functional and Lineage Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| **ZW-LEDG-MAP** | *Maputiro* (Pregnancy Damages) | Maternal Grandfather | Acknowledges biological paternity and compensates the family for the out-of-wedlock pregnancy. |
| **ZW-LEDG-CHI** | *Chiredzwa* (Maintenance/Rearing) | Maternal Grandfather | Reimburses the maternal family for the costs of raising and feeding the child. |
Once both *Maputiro* and *Chiredzwa* are settled, custody and the child's totem drift to the biological father's line. If these payments are unresolved, the child remains on the maternal grandfather's tree.
In statutory law, this customary barrier has been challenged. The High Court and Supreme Court of Zimbabwe (Sadiqi v Muteswa) ruled that the common law giving sole guardianship to the mother was unconstitutional, establishing that parents of children born in or out of wedlock can equally exercise **joint custody and guardianship** based on the best interests of the child.

### Customary Severance and Divorce

If the marriage dissolves due to misconduct, the husband can petition the traditional court to return a portion of the *Amalobolo* cattle. If the husband drives the woman away without a formal traditional hearing, the marriage remains valid.

## Deep Ancestral Overrides and Spiritual Restitution

* **Avenging Spirit (*Ngozi*) Restitution:** If an ancestor committed murder, the victim's family could demand a spiritual settlement (*Ngozi*). Historically, this involved paying cattle or, in some eras, a young woman to the victim's clan to balance the lineage network. Children born from these unions were structurally assigned to the **victim's family lineage tree**, bypassing the biological father's details.
* **Reviving the Extinguished Hearth (*Kumutsa Mapfihwa*):** If a married woman passed away without bearing children, her family line was customarily obligated to provide a younger sister or niece (*Chimutsamapfihwa*) to step into her place. Any children born to this new partnership are legally categorized as the children of the **deceased first wife**, keeping her specific branch on the tree active.
To prevent data errors during inheritance audits, the database engine implements a customary lineage override property, allowing researchers to differentiate between a node's biological parentage and their customary lineage anchor :

### WHO and Civil Registration and Vital Statistics (CRVS) Compliance
To support public health and cause-of-death (CoD) tracking in remote areas, the system matches UN CRVS standards. Birth and death registrations are logged using structured forms designed to be compatible with national vital statistics frameworks.
Cause-of-death mapping utilizes the **WHO International Classification of Diseases (ICD-11)**. The offline database contains a localized dictionary of ICD-11 diagnostic codes.
When a death event is registered, the local application uses **DORIS (Digital Open Rule Integrated System)** rule logic to validate the reported sequence of events, ensuring accurate identification of the underlying cause of death (UCOD) before compilation.

### Decentralized Vector Clock Conflict Resolution
When decentralized database files are shared between devices, conflicts are resolved using a **Vector Clock** model (V) to verify version history without a central server :
* Every database record contains a vector clock mapping devices to sequence numbers:
* When an incoming record is imported, the system evaluates the incoming clock (V_{\text{incoming}}) against the local clock (V_{\text{local}}):
   1. If V_{\text{incoming}} > V_{\text{local}} for all keys, the local record is automatically overwritten.
   2. If the vector clocks are concurrent (V_{\text{incoming}} \parallel V_{\text{local}}), indicating a merge conflict, the record is flagged as DISPUTED and queued for manual review by family elders or traditional council administrators.
### Offline Trust Graph (Consanguinity Verification)
Privacy controls for on-device media timelines utilize a recursive tree-traversal algorithm to calculate the exact degree of consanguinity (d) between the active user and the author of a post before displaying images :
```
                     [Active User Node]
                             │
                      (Step 1: Parent)
                             │
                             ▼
                       [Parent Node]
                             │
                    (Step 2: Grandparent)
                             │
                             ▼
                    [Grandparent Node]
                             │
                     (Step 3: Uncle)
                             │
                             ▼
                        [Uncle Node]
                             │
                     (Step 4: Cousin)
                             │
                             ▼
                    [Cousin Node (d = 4)]

```
The algorithm evaluates relationship distance metrics:
```javascript
function calculateConsanguinityDistance(startNodeId, targetNodeId, graph, visited = new Set(), depth = 0) {
    if (startNodeId === targetNodeId) return depth;
    if (depth > 4) return Infinity; // Limit traversal to 2nd cousins (d = 4)
   
    visited.add(startNodeId);
    const node = graph[startNodeId];
    let minDistance = Infinity;
   
    // Union parents, children, and spouses as valid structural adjacencies
    const adjacencies = [...node.parents,...node.children,...node.spouses];
   
    for (const adjacentId of adjacencies) {
        if (!visited.has(adjacentId)) {
            const distance = calculateConsanguinityDistance(adjacentId, targetNodeId, graph, visited, depth + 1);
            if (distance < minDistance) minDistance = distance;
        }
    }
   
    visited.delete(startNodeId);
    return minDistance;
}

```
Media files are tagged with visibility constraints. If the calculated path distance is less than or equal to the designated threshold:
the image is rendered on the client's timeline. This ensures intimate family media remains protected from the broader clan network entirely offline.
## Conclusions
To successfully build a genealogical platform for the Zimbabwean context, database architectures must transition away from standard Western genealogical models. By replacing linear structures with relational taxonomies that account for administrative geolocations, dual-tier totems, and customary inheritance laws, the platform can operate as a validated lineage registry.
This framework aligns with international metadata standards, including UNESCO’s CIDOC CRM , NAZ’s ISAD(G) , and WHO’s CRVS guidelines. Supported by an offline-first peer-to-peer sync engine, this architecture ensures that local oral histories and vital statistics can be preserved, synced, and validated directly within remote communities, bridging the gap between traditional heritage and global scientific databases.
