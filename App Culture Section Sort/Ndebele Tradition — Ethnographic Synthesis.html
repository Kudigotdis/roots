Ndebele Tradition

Marriage and kinship, totemic identity, pregnancy and childbirth, social etiquette, foodways, and mortuary practices.

At a glance

  • Ndebele identity is rooted in patrilineal clans (isibongo) and totems (isitemo) that define kinship, marriage eligibility, and belonging.
  • Marriage (lobola) is a negotiated process involving cattle, gifts, and the participation of extended family; it is understood as payment for offspring, not for the bride alone.
  • Pregnancy is honoured through ritual goats, herbal care, and delivery at the maternal grandmother's home, with distinct protocols for wedlock and out-of-wedlock births.
  • Ubuntu — humanness — underpins all social interaction, expressed through ukuhlonipha (respect), greetings, hospitality, and hierarchical deference.

01Marriage and LobolaCustomary law, negotiations, and ceremonial sequence
Customary Law Marriage

Lobola is the Ndebele customary-law marriage between a man and a woman, serving as the foundation of traditional union for generations. In modern times, couples often complement lobola with a Western-style church or civil ceremony, but the customary marriage remains paramount.

What is Lobola?

Lobola is the bride price or dowry a man pays to enter into traditional marriage. A man is not considered fully married until lobola has been paid in full. The price is traditionally paid in cattle or cash equivalent. Families agree on a price per cow, calculated against the number of cows charged by the bride's negotiating team. The process can take months and includes additional monetary payments and gifts to the bride's family.

Why Lobola is Necessary

Lobola formalizes the union between a man and a woman, bringing families together, establishing accountability, and providing recourse in case of marital difficulties. The process is broken into deliberate steps that build gradual relationships and trust between the two families.

In the past, when a woman could not conceive, lobola was used as a tool to return her to her family — the bride's family could physically return cows and money to the groom's family upon dissolution of the marriage. This reflects the core Ndebele understanding that lobola is paid for the children born into a marriage, not for the bride herself.

Key Participants

Idombo

The groom's chief negotiator — typically an uncle or trusted friend, never the father or a woman.

Obabomdala / Obabomncane

Paternal uncles (older and younger brothers of the father).

Omalume

Maternal uncles (mother's brothers).

Omamabadala / Omamabancane

Paternal aunts (older and younger sisters of the father).

Umakoti / Umlobokazi

The bride-to-be — not allowed to participate in actual negotiations.

Umkhwenyana / Umyeni

The groom-to-be — not allowed to participate in actual negotiations.

Declaration of Intentions

Before the lobola process begins, the bride and groom exchange gifts (engagement tokens). They then approach their respective aunt or uncle to present their intention to marry — each party informs their uncle/aunt in the absence of the other.

The groom's family asks for a meeting date with the bride's family. A trusted person (Idombo) is sent in a practice called ukucela umlilo — "seeking a relationship." The bride's family presents all the girls in the household so the messenger can identify the correct girl. She must produce a gift exchanged with the young man (a tie, small money, etc.) as proof of their relationship and his promise to marry her.

The Negotiations — Ukulobola
1

Arrival — Ukungena ekhaya

The groom's party arrives at the specified time. Punctuality is essential — lateness can incur fines as a sign of disrespect. The party stands outside the homestead until they pay a fee (ukungena ekhaya) to enter. They may be kept waiting for hours to test their resolve and demonstrate the value attached to the bride.

2

Announcing arrival

The party may shout their clan name and purpose at the gate. Announcing themselves more than once or too loudly can be deemed disrespectful and may result in fines.

3

Mouth Opener — IsiVulamlomo

A cow or cash fee is paid to "open the mouths" of the bride's negotiating team, allowing them to acknowledge and speak to the groom's family. In modern times, this fee may be broken into multiple payments: to see the father, to sit down, to bring in the bride, etc.

4

Introduction — Ukangaziwe

After payment, the Idombo declares the groom's intention to marry their daughter. The bride identifies the groom by producing his gift. A token in cash or livestock is paid before introductions proceed.

Payments and Ceremonial Items

Inkomo yohlanga

The "Mother's Cow" — a female cow given to the mother as appreciation for raising the bride. This cow is counted among the lobola cattle and must be "walking" (live), as it cannot be translated to cash equivalent due to its symbolism.

Ijazili kababa

A jacket for the bride's father.

Ingubo kamama

A blanket for the bride's mother, plus a complete outfit (shoes, dress, headpiece iqhiye or hat).

Groceries

Not a traditional requirement, but offered at the family's discretion. It may bring favour or displeasure, as it may be interpreted as implying they lack enough food.

Once part of the lobola is paid, the groom is formally introduced as umkhwenyana (son-in-law), and the bride is handed over to her in-laws. A celebration (umthimba) marks the union. If a Western wedding is desired, it may occur afterward. The couple is recognized as husband and wife once part of the lobola has been paid.

The bride lives with her husband even if lobola is not fully paid. Once a child is born, the bride's family can pressure the groom to complete payment, because "kulotshwa abantwana" — lobola is paid for the offspring. The lobola process is usually concluded when the first child is born.

02Clans and TotemsIsibongo, isitemo, kinship, and environmental guardianship
Isibongo — Patrilineal identity

Ndebele society is patriarchal, so a child inherits their father's surname (isibongo), which establishes the bloodline. The word derives from bonga (to thank, praise, or worship), meaning one draws pride and identity from their father's clan. A child must also adopt their father's totem (isitemo), another essential emblem of isibongo.

Totems — Isitemo

Totems are derived from animals, plants, and nature, with elements of ancestral names, physical power, appearance, and deeds. They symbolize harmony between humans, animals, and the environment, reflecting a clan's relationship with a specific animal.

Why totems matter

  • Establish family relations: Totems define bloodlines; people of a clan are generally related.
  • Symbols of clan pride: Comparable to European coats of arms or Scottish clan kilts.
  • Prevent incest: It is taboo to marry someone from your own clan, as they are considered of the same bloodline.
  • Environmental protection: Because clans had multiple totems, not every animal was hunted or harmed.
  • Oral tradition: Totem histories are passed down through stories, praise poems, and imbongi (praise poets).

Clan protocol and taboos

Historically, each member was responsible for physically protecting and defending their totem. This included not harming the animal or plant and actively nourishing, rescuing, or caring for it as needed. It remains taboo to consume an animal that is a symbol of one's totem. Totems are a source of identity, defining bloodlines and preventing incest — to this day, it is taboo to marry someone from your own clan, as custom considers them of the same bloodline.

03Pregnancy and ChildbirthWedlock protocols, out-of-wedlock, rituals, and postnatal care
Pregnancy in wedlock

Falling pregnant in wedlock is a welcome and eagerly awaited occurrence. When the couple realizes the bride is pregnant, several procedures follow:

1

Imbudzi yokhu Binga Isisu

The husband informs his wife's parents, bringing a female goat to be slaughtered and eaten by both parents. This goat "strengthens the mother's back" — it is believed that not slaughtering it will make the wife's mother weak and she might "break her back." The goat is partly ritualistic; in eating it, family elders educate the expectant mother on pregnancy and child care.

2

Herbal mixtures

During the first and second trimesters, skilled elderly women prepare herbal supplements to strengthen the expectant mother's body and prepare her for delivery.

3

Ukubvula Ndlela

Literally "opening the path/road" — a second-trimester massage therapy targeting the private parts to exercise muscles in readiness for childbirth. It is considered bad omen when a woman tears during delivery, so this massage is carried out until the final days of pregnancy.

4

Waiting for childbirth

As delivery approaches, the husband takes his wife to her mother's house, where she gives birth under the care of her experienced mother and aunts. The husband brings two goats of different sexes — one for celebration (slaughtered to welcome the newborn) and one for the mother's upkeep after birth (to boost milk production).

After birth — Ukucolewa

Two to three weeks after the child's birth, the father's family visits to see the baby for the first time (ukucolewa). They observe the child carefully to confirm if it is truly theirs, looking for similarities and birth marks found in their bloodline. The groom's family also decides whether the newborn and mother remain with the bride's mother or move back to the in-laws.

Marriage out of wedlock

Like other Nguni/Bantu tribes, the Ndebele value pregnancies after marriage. A child falling pregnant before marriage is often seen as a shame upon her parents. If this occurs, the girl's aunt and uncle inquire who impregnated her. Upon being informed, they take the pregnant girl to the impregnator's parents' house and leave her there. The boy's parents cannot chase her out, even if their son disagrees — accepting the pregnant girl symbolizes that pregnancy and childbirth should result from a committed relationship, not just lust.

Notably, the Imbudzi yokhu Binga Isisu is not required for out-of-wedlock pregnancies. After childbirth, the man must begin paying lobola and associated damages for impregnating someone before marriage.

04Manners, Protocols, and UbuntuRespect, hierarchy, greetings, and family etiquette
Ubuntu and Ukuhlonipha

Ubuntu — "Humanness"

Guided by the foundational principles of respect, politeness, obedience, warmth, empathy, and other moral values held high by society. Ukuhlonipha (respect) forms a significant aspect, representing acknowledging another person by subduing oneself and elevating the other (humility), reflected in bowing, kneeling, squatting, or avoiding eye contact with elders.

Politeness

Graciousness, civility, courtesy, respect, and empathy — "walking in another person's shoes."

Obedience

Adherence to set principles, values, and behavioural standards within society; following instructions of parents, elders, or supervisors.

Warmth

Empathy — feeling another's pain and wishing them well; cancels out selfishness.

Hierarchy

Relationships between young and old, royals and subjects, village elders, married and unmarried — determines how members interact and maintains orderliness.

Greetings

Greetings are a symbol of respect, empathy, and humility. It is fundamental for a well-mannered person to pass greetings and respond appropriately. Not greeting others is seen as a sign of disrespect and is not tolerated.

The younger person is expected to greet the older first. Elders may keep quiet until a younger person greets them — failure to do so is disrespectful.

Salibonani
Good morning / good afternoon / good evening — standard greeting.
Uvukenjani / Livukenjani
How did you sleep? (how are you this morning?) — U is singular, Li is plural (used for respect toward elders).
U(Li)itshonenjani
Good afternoon — how was your day?
U(Li)lale kuhle
Good night — sleep well.

Using the plural form (Li) when greeting an elder is expected; using the singular is considered impolite. This also applies to high-ranking members of society — royals, mayors, members of parliament, etc.

It is unacceptable to use a person's first name unless you are familiar with them and of the same age group. Children are prohibited from mentioning an elder's first name. Elders with children are referred to by their children's names — e.g., "mother of Bekhezile" or "father of Zwelihle."

Guests and hospitality

The Ndebele place a premium on treating visitors well, as this reflects family values and can determine how society views the entire extended family. All guests are welcome and treated with utmost respect.

  • Visitors are expected to bring a present or token — a sign of thoughtfulness and appreciation.
  • Women generally join the matriarch to assist with meal preparation, table setting, or cleaning up.
  • It is considered polite to eat whatever the host provides (whether you feel full or not) and not to be wasteful, as this reflects your family's values.
Manners within the family

Eye contact

Never look elders in the eyes — it is seen as a challenge to authority and undermining the elderly.

Addressing siblings

Brothers and sisters are umnewethu and udadewethu. Elder brothers are referred to as ubhudi followed by their name.

Responses

One may answer "we-e-e!" to younger siblings. Parents always respond "we-e-e!" to their children regardless of age — symbolizing hierarchy.

Relatives

Father's sister: Babakazi; mother's brother: Malume. Father's brothers are Baba; mother's sisters are Ma — all elders are one's parents.

Grandparents

Both maternal and paternal grandparents are Babamkhulu or Khulu (grandfather) and Gogo (grandmother).

Girl children

Traditionally, a girl child was expected to kneel when talking to her father and not look any elder directly in the eyes.

  • Receive and give items with both hands — using one hand is disrespectful. Always use the right hand; the left hand carries bad luck.
  • Children must not point a finger at anybody older than them.
  • Do not eat while standing — respect the cook by sitting down; men must remove hats.
  • Children in the company of elders must be quiet, not start conversations, and go outside rather than disturb elders' discussions.
05Food, Dining, and HospitalityStaples, recipes, beverages, and dining etiquette
Meal preparation and division of labour

Traditionally, men gathered firewood, cut it to size, slaughtered animals, or went hunting. Women, assisted by young girls, prepared meals. Meal times were daily family gatherings. With modern influences, the evening meal may be the only time the family gathers together.

When guests visit, their needs are prioritized over those of the household. Families often prepare more than enough food. Guests receive the best food and drink. Women take out their best kitchenware — not to show off, but to show gratitude and value for the guest. This is part of Ubuntu hospitality.

Breakfast

A typical Ndebele breakfast consists of hot tea (plain or with milk) and homemade bread. Some may prefer lemon juice or coffee. Since the Ndebele are traditionally agriculturalists, they kept free-range chickens, and eggs could be added to breakfast — though not regularly, to allow chickens to multiply.

Sometimes a light cornmeal porridge (plain or with milk) is served, or soft porridge from pounded sorghum, especially during ploughing season when manual labour requires lasting energy. This soft porridge is also consistently served to new mothers to boost milk production.

Lunch and dinner — Isitshwala

The Ndebele staple food is maize/corn, which can be eaten alone or with other ingredients. The main starch is isitshwala, a thick cornmeal porridge.

Isitshwala recipe

Ingredients: Maize meal (imphuphu) and water.

  1. Boil 3 cups of water in a large saucepan.
  2. Mix one quarter maize meal with a cup of water in a mixing bowl until you get a thick paste.
  3. Add boiling water to the paste, constantly stirring with a wooden spoon to avoid lumps and sticking.
  4. Add a tablespoon of margarine (optional).
  5. Cover and let boil for 10–15 minutes over medium heat.
  6. Gradually add remaining maize meal, mixing vigorously to blend.
  7. Cover and simmer for 5–10 more minutes over low heat.
  8. Serve with green vegetables and beef/chicken stew or curdled (sour) milk.

Amahewu — traditional fermented drink

Amahewu recipe

Ingredients: 100g maize meal, 4 litres water, 100g sorghum with finger millet malt. (Leftover isitshwala can also be used.)

  1. Mix dry ingredients in a pot with a little water to create a paste.
  2. Boil 2½ litres of water and add to the paste while stirring to prevent curdling.
  3. Bring to a boil over medium heat, then cover and simmer for about 30 minutes.
  4. Remove from heat and allow to cool.
  5. Add remaining water cold to thin to a drinkable consistency.
  6. Cover and place in a warm, dry place for 3–4 days to brew (some put it in the sun). Stir at least once a day.
  7. After 4 days, amahewu is ready. Serve chilled or at room temperature; add sugar to taste. Makes about 4 litres.
06Death, Funerals, and MourningAnnouncement, preparation, burial protocols, and community roles
Death announcement

Death is treated as a community responsibility. In the olden days, elders (assisted by elderly neighbours) certified death. The immediate family is informed first, then extended family, who in turn inform neighbours and distant relatives. Today, telephones and social media accelerate the announcement; some families even post through local radio stations.

Preparations for the funeral

In the past, when there were no mortuaries, the corpse was buried within 24 hours. Those who couldn't arrive in time missed the burial but could participate in later rituals like ukuphosa ilitshe (throwing a stone on the grave).

Today, with mortuaries and extended families living far away, a typical funeral can take a week or longer. During the week preceding burial, community members gather daily for prayers, console the bereaved, and share stories about the deceased as counseling. The chief mourner's will is taken into account, though family elders may override it.

Burial protocols
1

Gravesite selection

Identified by men in the family (sometimes with the chief mourner's advice) in the early morning on the day of burial.

2

Grave digging

Strictly men, with the help of neighbours. An elder inspects for depth and size (determined by the deceased's size or casket).

3

Guarding the grave

It is taboo to leave the dug grave alone — some diggers remain to guard it until the mourners arrive.

4

Division of labour

Men dig and cover the grave; women collect stones to construct and strengthen the tomb/grave top.

Burial timing and categories

Head of the family (older man)

Buried in the early afternoon (just before lunch), never in the morning.

Woman / mother

Buried in the morning hours before noon, close to the isiphala (grain hut).

Other family members

Buried at any time of the day.

In the olden days, the Ndebele wrapped their dead in blankets or animal hides; graves were oblong and proportionate to the deceased's size. Today, caskets are used, and graves are sized accordingly.

Funeral procession and burial

With cars and caskets, the journey to the graveyard is shorter. The hearse carrying the corpse leads the procession, with the husband/wife/parent of the deceased also in the hearse, followed by the mourners. Hymns are sung as the casket is lowered. After burial, most community members return home, while elders and family remain to clean up and stay with the bereaved as they adapt to life without their loved one.

Context note: Ndebele practices are not uniform. They vary by clan, locality, generation, family circumstance, and religious affiliation. This page is a structured synthesis of the supplied material, not a claim that every practice is universal or unchanged.

Glossary

Lobola
The Ndebele customary-law marriage; also the bride price or dowry paid in cattle or cash to formalize union and secure offspring.
Isibongo
Surname or clan name, derived from the father's bloodline; source of pride and identity.
Isitemo
Totem — an animal, plant, or natural symbol that defines a clan's identity, prohibits inter-clan marriage, and connects humans to the environment.
Ubuntu
"Humanness" — a philosophy of respect, politeness, obedience, warmth, empathy, and interdependence.
Ukuhlonipha
Respect — a guiding principle expressed through humility, deference to elders, and proper social conduct.
Isitshwala
Thick cornmeal porridge, the staple starch of Ndebele meals, served with relish or stew.
Amahewu
A non-alcoholic fermented drink made from maize meal, sorghum, and malt; often served to new mothers and at communal gatherings.

Idombo
The groom's chief negotiator during lobola — typically an uncle or trusted friend.
Ukucolewa
The postnatal visit by the father's family to see the newborn, usually two to three weeks after birth.

Integrated cultural life

Across marriage, totemic identity, pregnancy, etiquette, food, and death, the traditions described here connect social belonging with ancestral responsibility, collective care, and the enduring philosophy of Ubuntu.
